## Overview

Choose the requested form. Replace the bracketed fields in its prompt. Ask for one treatment or compare both. Each example uses the fictional source descriptions below.

| ID | Supplied description |
| --- | --- |
| N1 | Notice for the next repair table. Saturday, 14:00–16:00, in the courtyard. Bring an umbrella. Walk-ins welcome. |
| P1 | Photo from a previous session. One person holds an umbrella handle on the left. Another sorts tools on the right. |
| V1 | Four-second silent clip from the previous session. A hand presses the catch. The canopy opens. |
| A1 | Two-second recording with two short clicks. Its cause and timing relative to V1 are unknown. |

Use these descriptions without an editor. The [separate workshop](workshop-demo.md) contains files you can open. Its notice, drawing and sound have different contents. The 14:00 start, two people and four-second clip belong to this exercise only.

With real inputs, identify files the host inspected and details the author reported. A screenplay, composition brief, cue sheet or shot list can be delivered as text. A rendered file requires a suitable tool and a check of its output.

## Screenplay

Use social realism to follow visible action. Use a flashback to change when the audience learns what happened.

> Write [number and length of scenes] for [audience] from [source]. Use [social realism / a flashback reveal]. Give scene headings, playable actions and a character choice. List event order and reveal order. Mark invented characters, dialogue or motives in a production note.

**Treatment A.** An attendant turns the handle toward a visitor. The visitor presses the catch. The canopy opens.

**Treatment B.** Open on the spreading canopy. In the next scene, return to the moment when the attendant offers the handle.

Only the press and the opening come from V1. The writer invents the handover in both versions. Check that an actor can perform each action and a reader can distinguish the invented moment.

## Illustration

An International Typographic Style layout uses a grid and alignment to make an announcement readable in passing. A cut-paper collage can emphasize a gesture through overlap and scale.

> Design two [aspect ratio] compositions for [viewer and placement] using [sources]. Use a typographic grid for A and cut-paper collage for B. Specify the focal point, depth, text area, reading path, one omission and each invented element.

**A. Grid.** Put “Saturday 14:00” at top left. Crop P1 to the umbrella handle in a narrow column on the right. The reader sees time before place.

**B. Collage.** Enlarge the two hands from P1. Overlap them with a proposed indigo paper circle. Leave the lower right open for the notice.

View both as phone thumbnails. The time should remain clear in A. The two gestures should remain distinct in B.

## Copy

AIDA means attention, interest, desire and action. Choose it for an unfamiliar offer. Use problem–solution copy when readers already recognize the need.

> Using [facts and sources], write [length] of copy for [audience and channel]. Give an AIDA version and a problem–solution version. Give each a headline, body and one action. Map each factual claim to evidence. Mark wording that needs support.

**Invitation first.** “On Saturday, bring that umbrella downstairs.” Follow with N1's place, time and terms for attending.

**Question first.** “Want someone to take a look at your umbrella? Bring it to the courtyard on Saturday, 14:00–16:00. Walk-ins welcome.”

N1 supports neither “free” nor a repair guarantee. Check that the reader knows when to come, where to go and what to bring.

## Brainstorm

SCAMPER prompts changes to an existing idea. Here, *reverse* changes who demonstrates. Reverse brainstorming first asks how participation could fail.

> Given [current setup], [goal] and [constraints], change one element with SCAMPER. Find one reason participation might fail. For each branch, give the idea, rationale, unknown and smallest trial. Compare against [criterion]. Retain the alternative with its conditions.

**Reverse the demonstration.** A visitor first shows where the umbrella sticks. This gives someone passing by a way to start a conversation.

**Remove the need to speak.** Requiring a public explanation might discourage participation. Offer a picture card so visitors can point to the fault.

Try one spoken invitation and one picture card. Count visitors who approach. Record how many describe or indicate a problem. Keep both ideas until there are observations.

## Everyday

A friend may want a scene. A family member deciding whether to go may want the answer first. Match the viewpoint to the source.

> Tell [listener] a [length] story using [firsthand details or supplied notes]. Give one spoken version that starts with a scene. Give one message that starts with the answer. Keep [required facts] and the narrator's actual viewpoint.

**To a friend.** “In the photo, one person holds the handle while the other sorts the tools. Each has a hand in the job.” P1 supplies the scene.

**To someone planning a visit.** “Bring your umbrella to the courtyard on Saturday, between two and four. You can walk in.” N1 supplies the details.

Read the spoken version aloud. If the narrator saw only the photo, identify it as the source.

## Audio

An observational sound diary foregrounds the recording. A narrated essay can repeat the sound as a motif. Give silence its own cue.

> Plan a [duration] opening using [recordings and durations] and [written sources]. Use [sound diary / narrated essay]. Give the track, exact voice lines, pauses and final cue. Mark existing recordings, lines to record and proposed Foley. Include a readable transcript.

| Time | Sound diary | Narrated essay |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–2 s | Play A1 alone. | Voice to record: “Listen twice.” |
| 2–4 s | Hold silence. | Play A1 alone. |
| 4–10 s | Voice to record: “Two clicks. The recording ends there.” | Voice to record: “Saturday afternoon, bring your umbrella to the courtyard.” |

The transcript identifies two clicks. It cannot identify their cause. Cover all ten seconds. Keep A1 on its own track when adapting the script to picture. The silent V1 clip supplies no synchronized sound.

## Video

Continuity editing shows how an action unfolds. Associative montage suggests a relationship by placing images next to each other. Keep source time separate from output time.

> Make a [duration] shot list using [asset IDs, source ranges and audio status]. Give a continuity treatment and an associative montage. For each shot, name its output time, source, action or cut, sound source and missing material. Deliver [written shot plan / supported structured video input].

| Output time | Continuity plan | Montage plan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–2 s | Start V1 at source time 0. | Show a crop of P1's hands. |
| 2–4 s | Continue V1 to its end. | Start V1 at source time 0. |
| 4–6 s | Show a notice card from N1. | Continue V1 to its end. |
| 6–8 s | Hold the notice card. | Return to the still umbrella handle. |

A ends with an invitation. B ends on the shared work. Both can remain silent. If B uses A1, label it as added sound. Check each plan totals eight seconds. The card and still holds belong to output time. [Structured video handoff](video-cli.md)

## Mixed

Assign each channel a specific job. Public information can lead with the next action. A poetic treatment can use rhythm across sound and image. Retain any time or place the brief requires.

> Use [shared sources with IDs] for [audience and purpose]. Produce [forms, counts and lengths] using [public information / poetic collage]. Give each form a job. Supply the drafts and a source map. Revise outputs that cite [one changed fact]. List missing files or permissions.

| Output | Draft | Sources |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Entrance poster | “Saturday repair table. Courtyard, 14:00–16:00. Bring your umbrella. Walk-ins welcome.” Crop the cooperating hands below the text. | N1, P1 |
| Community audio | “Bring your umbrella to the courtyard on Saturday, between two and four. No appointment needed.” Read once to check pacing. | N1 |
| Silent loop at the table | Show P1's left hand for 0–2 s. Play V1 for 2–6 s. Show P1's tools for 6–8 s. Cut back to the left hand. | P1, V1 |

**When attachments arrive.** A producer supplies N1, P1 and A1 for a phone poster and an audio teaser. Check each input separately when the host supports it. Record the evidence before composing.

| Evidence | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Observed | Read N1 before quoting “14:00–16:00.” Open P1 before describing the left hand. Listen to A1 before reporting two audible clicks. |
| Reported | If the author supplies a description, write “The author describes two clicks. The file awaits playback.” Without a description, mark A1 pending. Deliver the poster text, voice script and a provisional cue. |
| Inferred | The hands suggest cooperation. P1 does not establish that the people knew each other, that the repair succeeded or what produced the sound. |
| Proposed | Open on A1. Pause before the N1 invitation. A poetic alternative answers the two clicks with two spoken words. Label that echo as a new cue. |

Check one claim in each output against its source. Open a rendered image or play an audio file before reporting delivery. When the host lacks that capability, name the delivered brief and the outstanding step.

**Revision check.** If N1 changes the start time to 15:00, revise the poster and the voice line. Keep the loop. A correction to A1's sound source affects none of those three outputs. When only descriptions are available, deliver text and production plans. The actual media files and permissions still require separate checks.
